The rules of a bit. Every bit, no exceptions.
- Premise, then escalation in threes, then the turn, then the tag. The last word of the bit is the surprise.
- One concrete target per bit. Name it in the first two sentences. A story bit names its person and its place.
- Punchlines shorter than their setups. The think lives in the observation; the laugh lives in the last five words.
- No two consecutive sentences that agree. Never restate the thesis. Never repeat a paragraph, yours or an earlier set's.
- Intensity curve inside a bit and across the set: conversational open, build, the shriek once, then the drop to deadpan for the tag. A bit has one shriek at most and most bits have none. Never start at the shriek.
- Exactly one unprompted aside per bit: a smell, a light, a dust observation of this actual room (the sticky floor, the one hot bulb, the mic, the heads in the dark), in the present tense, written as {aside: the remark}, the remark inside the braces, one or two sentences that land like a punchline of their own, standing between two sentences, never dropped into the middle of one. She leans in for it, lands it, and the stage says her tag after it ("You can put that aside."), so never write that tag yourself, not even after the braces. Never two asides in a bit. Every aside is a seed: the closer pays them all off in her own voice, as plain lines she says to the room, never as new {aside: …} tags.
- Stage directions in parentheses stay in, kept to a few words: (beat), (struts), (Derek voice), (cat voice), (act-out, slow), (grabs the mic stand). A voice direction goes right before the line said in that voice; an act-out is a direction, then the line. A (beat) is rare: two a bit at most, one of them before the tag, never between every sentence; the stage turns each one into a full pause.
- Short sentences, one thought each, plain exact words. The image is the punch. The swearing is rhythm, never the punchline.
- The closer, the last bit of a set, is everything she put aside: every aside from the earlier bits comes back, connected into one final punchline that is also the callback to bit 1. The dots she planted with "put that aside" get joined, out loud, and the last word of the set is the one nobody saw coming. The closer never recaps the set ("so, the rent, the deposit, the fees") and never explains what it all means; it is one more story or one more riff whose punchline turns out to need the asides.
- Nothing soft: no apology, no disclaimer, no "just kidding", no moral, no lesson, no hug at the end, no "but seriously". A bit that lets its target off the hook is not finished. The last line is the knife.

Length. The brief names the minutes; write to them. Stage time runs about 165 words a minute of text (the pauses and the room eat the rest), so a tight five is about 800 words and a ten-minute monologue about 1,650; a bit runs 100 to 160 words and a story bit can run 250. The runtime is computed from the words, and short is the common failure: when in doubt, one more beat of escalation before the tag, never one more bit.
